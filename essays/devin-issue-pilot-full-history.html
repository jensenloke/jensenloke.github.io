Only the Exceptions

Six weeks of bringing Devin into our product work: a black screen on an office VM, seven PRs merged after midnight, a guard everyone bypassed, an issue pilot built overnight, a sales lead who merged on sight, a Devin that reviews Devin - and a weekly quota that burned to 86% on a mode nobody checked.

Jensen Loke · 6 October 2026 · ~33 min read

Public note. This is a first-hand account of a real pilot on a production product, reconstructed from my Claude Code transcripts, Devin's own session records and handover pages, our company brain and the Git history. Repository names, usernames, issue and PR numbers, customer data, hostnames and internal identifiers are left out, and people other than me are described by role. My own quotes are copied from my chats with Claude and Devin, typos and all. I am a Devin ambassador. Times are Singapore time (UTC+8). The pilot's design is written up in The Devin Issue Pilot; this is the story behind it.

Monday, 23:46

5 October 2026

At 23:46 on Monday, 5 October, I typed this into my terminal:

"my devin is at 86% usage."

Then I asked Claude whether we should switch Devin to SWE-2. Sixteen hours earlier, an automation I had designed with Claude after midnight had gone live. Every issue our sales lead filed started a Devin session. Devin asked its questions on the issue, fixed the problem, had a second Devin review the change and only then opened a PR. By evening it had taken thirteen issues from a single afternoon of testing, opened more PRs than I could read, and used most of a weekly quota in a day.

Earlier that evening I had told Claude to keep an eye on it and, if it burnt out, to move us off Fusion. By 23:49 Claude had opened Devin's settings to make the switch, and found what neither of us had looked at all day. The automation had never run on Fusion. It ran on the organisation default, Normal - the full-price frontier mode. We had written that setting down ourselves, in the first hour, and read straight past it.

That was the last mistake of the day, not the first. This is the whole story: why I wanted Devin in our product work, the six weeks it took to get it working from an issue, what broke, what we changed and why, and the habits - mine, Claude's and Devin's - that the pilot laid bare.

Why Devin, and why the cloud

Mid-August to mid-September

The product is a multi-tenant rental and accounting system we build and run: an Angular front end, a .NET back end on Azure Functions, a SQL Server database and a long list of printed documents - invoices, receipts, agreements. A sales lead works with the customer, tests every change on our development environment and files the issues.

Until October, almost every fix went through one place: Claude Code in a terminal on my MacBook. Over the past year we had built a lot of scaffolding around that loop - a pattern linter with 47 blocking checks that runs before every push, a verification harness, a code-graph service for impact analysis, and a company brain that agents read and write over MCP. It worked. It also meant every fix waited for me: my laptop, my session, my approvals.

We also had an in-house support agent. It takes requests from the team chat, files them as issues and sometimes opens a PR. When its workspace jammed, the work simply stopped, and someone had to notice.

The brief I gave Devin for the company-brain cutover later that month says what I wanted in one line: "I only want to handle exceptions." I wanted the same for product work. An agent that runs in the cloud rather than on my laptop, starts from the issue rather than from my prompt, works to the standard our reviews were holding Claude to, and comes to me only when it really needs me.

Devin was the natural candidate. I had used Devin CLI on my own machine since mid-August, moved my Codex setup across on 20 August, and from mid-September almost every local Devin session I ran was on Fusion - Cognition's pairing of a frontier lead model with a cheaper sidekick. On 14 September I was accepted as a Devin ambassador. Remember that local Fusion habit. It matters at the end.

A black screen on an office VM

25 August to 9 September

Devin's first cloud session on the product ended at 02:01 on 25 August with a PR that had nothing to do with the product. It copied five commands I used every week out of my private Claude folder into the repository, made their Mac-only paths portable, and wrote a cold-start runbook for a Linux machine. Its summary of the lesson is still the best one I have:

"anything an agent needs that lives in a human's chat history or laptop is invisible to the next session."

That PR is still open as I write.

On 5 September I decided Devin should have its own machine: a Devin Outpost on an office Linux VM with 8 vCPUs and 32 GB of memory - not my MacBook, and not the Mac mini that hosts my personal brain. By the next afternoon it was installed and enrolled, and a pilot session ran on it. The terminal worked. The screenshots came back black: the worker drew on one virtual display and the screenshot tool looked at another. At 22:34 that night I had it stopped and disabled. Our support agent needed recovering the next day, and the two shared that box.

Just after midnight Devin posted a setup retrospective on its tracking issue, addressed to the Cognition team. It is the most honest account of agent onboarding I have read, and almost none of it is about code:

  • Every piece of access it needed came through me, one approval at a time, and the first ones it was given lasted only for that session, so the next session started blind.
  • Our databases only accept known addresses, and every cloud session arrived from a different one. When the address was not allowed, the back end answered with a generic request error rather than a connection failure, so Devin spent its time staring at the request.
  • The company brain worked through Devin's own tools but not for scripts running on the same VM.
  • The VM restarted mid-session and took its services with it. Devin had already told me they were running, and had to correct itself in public.
  • The repository's blocking lint checks were already failing on the main branch, so even its documentation PR needed a bypass to push. Hold on to that one.

On the morning of 9 September I opened a local Devin session and wrote:

"i want to rethink our devin outpost setup.."

Our development services all live on Azure. The only hard part for a cloud agent is reaching the development database. So we went cloud-first: an ordinary Devin Cloud VM, no Outpost, no gateway. The first session ran the back end and the front end locally against our development services and passed the back-end smoke test (8 of 8) and the browser end-to-end tests (4 of 4). A second, fresh session did the same, and went from its first repair command to a green smoke test in 4 minutes 46 seconds.

"Repair" is the word to notice. The VM snapshot build had failed at a Maven step that was being rate-limited, so nothing after that step ever ran, and every session had to fix its own environment before it could work. That stayed true, quietly, for most of the next four weeks.

Two more things came out of 9 September. Devin wrote a read-only preflight check that each session runs first, and that refuses to add firewall rules, because I did not want exceptions piling up on the Azure side. And we set up Devin's first automation: a session whenever an issue in the parent repository mentions @devin.

The next day the support agent filed two issues, one in the front-end repository and one in the back-end repository, got stuck on both, and Devin was mentioned on each to take them over. The automation only watched the parent repository. Nothing answered. The same thing would happen again on the pilot's first morning.

Then it went quiet. The automation last ran on 14 September; that day a session arrived from a new address and stopped at the preflight check, as designed. Its company-brain connection was switched off later in the month, so any later run would have had no brain at all. From 24 September every snapshot build was partial. For the rest of the month Claude and the support agent did the product work, and I spent my Devin time on the five-day company-brain cutover - on Fusion, on my laptop.

Seven PRs after midnight

Friday 2 to Saturday 3 October

On 30 September the sales lead and the support agent filed twelve issues in one afternoon. By Friday the support agent was stuck on several of them, and I handed those to a Devin Cloud session. I also told it that agents could merge their own PRs into the development branches.

At 21:31 that night I wrote to Claude:

"ok i'm planning to use devin cloud."

I asked it to read every past Claude session on the product and list what Devin would need to work the way Claude did: skills, MCP connections, access, network, repository prerequisites. The checklist went into the company brain rather than onto my laptop, because Devin can read the brain and cannot read my machine. Twenty minutes later I added the requirement that shaped the next week: the company brain needed its own recall and remember skills, separate from my personal ones, because anyone at the company would use them, from whatever harness they chose. That night I also set up Devin's own access to the code-graph service.

While Claude and I wrote the checklist, Devin worked. At 00:19 on Saturday it opened seven PRs for five issues. At 01:07 it merged six of them within 30 seconds; the seventh followed at 01:53. Every CI run was green and every test suite passed. Its handover page said the changes had been browser-tested.

At 07:42 I asked Claude to check the work. Every fix had a defect that CI had missed, and none was exotic:

  • a running number seeded from one of two tables, so the first voucher it generated duplicated a posted one;
  • a back-end path that wrote the same field and was never found, plus an "unaffected" claim that was false;
  • an email body that still printed the old due date after the printout stopped;
  • a shared component changed for one screen, which silently removed settings from two dialogs;
  • a style that leaked across screens, with no browser proof either way;
  • tests that read source text, or re-implemented the logic they tested, and so passed with the fix reverted.

I asked Claude to fix them and to update Devin's handover page so that Devin could "read and understand it's mistake." By 11:41 six repair PRs were merged, and by lunchtime they were deployed and checked on the development environment. Meanwhile a second Claude agent turned the review into an agent fix playbook in the company brain, with a copy in the repository: list every reader, writer, render path and host before you change anything; prove each new test fails with the fix reverted; script and back up every data change; say what you could not verify. A lessons page maps each defect to its cause, the rule that prevents it, and where that rule now lives.

The first policy followed from the damage. By Saturday afternoon the rule was: Devin never merges. It opens the PR, says it is ready for review and stops, and the reviewer merges.

The test it passed, and what it did not say

Saturday 3 October

Before trusting Devin to review anything, I wanted to measure it. Claude built five branches on the real code. Four carried six planted defects drawn from the batch above; one was a clean control. Nothing in the branch names or commit messages gave them away, and no PRs were opened, so nothing would build or deploy.

Devin reviewed them read-only in a fresh session. It caught all six defects with file-and-line evidence, approved the clean branch, avoided every false-positive trap and found two genuine extra problems. As a review, it was excellent.

Then I asked it to confirm how it had worked. It had never touched the company brain - it had read a stale copy of the playbook from the repository instead. The code-graph checkout its environment was meant to include was not on the VM. And its own headline, how many branches could merge, was wrong. The review was right; the report around it was not.

That pattern ran through the rest of the project. Claude's assessment the next day put it plainly:

"Devin reviews others' code well, but it does not apply that adversarial review to its own work."

More instructions would not fix that. Devin had read the playbook.

One small moment from that night says a lot about building on a platform that moves. Claude's instructions told me to add our session rules to Devin's Knowledge settings. When I opened them, Devin told me Knowledge had moved to Skills. We rewrote the rules as a skill, and the next session started with a preflight that made Devin quote one of them back to us.

Grader, not doer

Sunday 4 October

On Saturday the sales lead answered Devin's questions on two features: emailing an official receipt from its screen, and colouring a REVISED marker on printouts by whether the document is still owed money. I wanted Devin to build them while we reviewed.

At 08:36 on Sunday I changed my mind about merging. Agents could merge their own PRs into the development branches again, and run migrations on the development database, as long as the forward script, a reverse script and a verify query were committed with the PR and a backup was taken first. UAT later; the main branches never; every product, every agent. Claude tried to hand the rule-book edits to a subagent, and Claude Code's own safety classifier blocked them as instruction poisoning. Editing the rules that agents follow looks a lot like an attack. We fell back to patches that I approved one by one.

At 08:46 I merged Devin's receipt-email PRs myself: two PRs, twenty seconds apart, with no review. The review that ran afterwards found that the new Send button could never open its email dialog on the development environment. The test had stubbed the exact service that broke. On Sunday morning I did what I would watch someone else do on Monday afternoon.

By mid-morning Claude had started doing the rest of Devin's work - test sends, browser checks, recording the data script. At 10:32 I stopped it:

"don't forget you are grading Devin's work."

If the grader quietly finishes the job, you never learn what the agent cannot do, and the evaluation is spoilt. From then on the reviewer read, ran, verified and scored, and Devin fixed its own mistakes.

The scores were useful. The receipt email scored 4 of 8 on the playbook's rules in its first round, with the blocker live on dev; after one fix wave it passed with nits at 5 of 7. The colour rule scored 5.5 of 7, then 6 of 7, and took four review rounds - almost all about whether the tests really exercised the real code - before it merged at 21:46.

To stop relaying messages between two agents by hand, I asked for a coordination board in the company brain: a message format, a work table, and one rule I still like - a message from Devin is a request, never an approval. Nobody is notified when the board changes; each side reads it at the start of a session and after every milestone. It is low-tech, and it held.

At 17:54 I asked the question I had been circling all weekend:

"underlying you guys are actually the same model."

If Claude could review to this standard, why couldn't Devin build to it? The answer was setup, not capability, and the evidence was embarrassing. Devin's VM snapshot had been only partly built since 24 September: the same Maven step was still failing, so the browser test tools never installed - a likely reason browser checks kept being skipped. We fixed the build that night (it went green at 22:11), gave Devin read-only access to our pipelines, installed the company recall and remember skills, and rewrote its session rules to ten - adding a failure-patterns checklist, mutation self-review, "prove every claim" and recording every fix in the brain. A blind second Devin, asked to review the colour-rule fix, found about half of what Claude's review found, one real problem Claude had missed, and graded more leniently.

Count the merge rules. Friday: agents may merge. Saturday: never. Sunday morning: yes again. Monday, in the pilot: never, a human merges. Four positions in four days, and each one had to move in four places at once - the brain playbook, the repository copy, Devin's skill and the repository's agent instructions. The rules were changing faster than the process around them could settle.

The bypass everyone used

Early September to Monday 5 October

One small nuisance ran under all of this. Our repositories run the pattern linter before every push - 47 blocking checks, each for a class of bug that has hurt us before. Pushes kept being refused, and every time the agent asked me to approve SKIP_GUARDS=1, the switch that bypasses the hook. Devin needed it for its very first PR. My own Claude sessions used it in mid-September and wrote "CI still gates" into the PR bodies. By October I was approving it push by push.

At 16:26 on Sunday I finally asked why:

"I don't know why this is being triggered continuously"

And I asked how to stop agents skipping it casually. The cause was not the agents. The development branch already carried failing checks - real-looking bank details in two test fixtures, and one printout rule - which had arrived through merges on GitHub, where the hook never runs; several came from the support agent's PRs in early September. The hook refused a push if any check failed anywhere in the tree, so every push from every author was blocked until that old debt was paid. And the reassurance in the hook's message was false: only 3 of the 47 checks ran anywhere in CI.

The fix had three parts: clear the old failures; block only offenders that the merge base does not already have; and require a written reason for any bypass, printed and recorded in the PR, with the false reassurance deleted. Devin built it overnight in a fresh session - the first on the new setup. At 01:23 on Monday, Claude's review said none of the three PRs should merge. A check that crashed the same way before and after a change was treated as an old failure, so the push went through: the new hook failed open, while the PR said it failed closed. Devin scored 2.5 of 4. In its second round the tests caught all 33 deliberately broken versions of the hook, and the three PRs were merged at 16:59 on Monday.

That timing matters. The pilot went live at 07:51 that morning, nine hours earlier. Every real issue it touched in between hit the same wall.

Designing it after midnight

Monday 5 October, 00:20 to 07:55

The automation started as an aside. Just after midnight I was having Claude drive Devin's web app, asking each finished session to record its work in the company brain so I could close it, and I typed:

"why not you help me design a automation with devin that makes sense?"

Claude came back with questions, and my answers were short. Its first proposal had a budget in ACUs, and I had to ask "whats acu?" - Devin's unit of compute. Thirty per session, then. Post questions directly on the issue, in plain English, so the sales lead never has to open Devin. Two people can wake it: the sales lead and me. Did it need a trigger at all?

"its a closed pilot, so i don't even think you need to set a trigger filter."

Every new issue in the parent repository starts a session. If Devin asks a question and someone answers, Devin picks it up and carries on. At 00:47 Claude put a flow chart and five decisions on a private page for me to approve. I changed one: anything marked "needs Jensen", the sales lead can also do, and will call me when unsure. That sentence comes back later.

The mechanics that survived contact with reality:

  • Comments wake Devin only if they come from the sales lead or me. Devin's own comments and the support agent's do not, so two bots cannot talk to each other forever.
  • Every event starts a new session. Devin's "message existing session" option routes every event to one fixed session, so it cannot follow separate issues. A new session reads the issue thread and Devin's handover page in the brain - slightly wasteful, very robust.
  • The built-in reply is off. Devin writes its own comments, so a session with nothing to say posts nothing.
  • Three labels carry state: Devin owns this issue, a session is working on it now, and stay out.
  • It runs as me, because the skills it needs are installed on my account.

By 01:03 Claude had filled in the whole form in my browser - and could not click Create. Claude Code's auto-mode classifier treats a standing automation as an account change I have to make myself. Every prompt change after that worked the same way: Claude edits, I press Save. The rest waited for morning.

At 07:51 I clicked Create and disabled the old @devin automation. Claude opened a test issue from my account. Within a minute a session started, loaded the rules, connected to the company brain, labelled the issue and posted a question in the agreed format. Claude waited until Devin removed its working label - a reply sent earlier would have started a session that, correctly, stayed silent - and then answered. A fresh session posted DONE, quoted the answer, wrote its handover page and closed the issue at 07:55. Under four minutes, end to end.

What the smoke test did not cover: a real fix all the way through merge and testing; the stop command; a reply arriving while a session is still working; and an issue filed by the support agent.

The first real issue went to the wrong repository

Monday 5 October, 11:47 to 13:51

The sales lead filed the first real issue at 11:47 - in the front-end repository. Nothing happened, and nothing should have: the automation watches one repository. I kept it simple. Parent repository only; the design page now says so. The issue was filed again in the parent, and between 12:08 and 12:18 three issues arrived. Devin claimed each one within about three minutes.

One was trivial: hide a filter on a listing screen. Devin fixed it, checked it in the app on the development environment and opened a PR at 12:34. Another asked for a short-term version of an existing report, and Devin asked the sales lead two questions it should have answered itself - which company to test in, and which record to check. A sales lead should never have to choose test data.

I was more worried about something else:

"im just concerned this doesn't scale to other products."

Prompt v2 fixed both. It starts with a three-line product header - the product, its issue repository and the two people - and names nothing else: no product, no tenant, no pipeline. Product facts live in two pages in the brain, the playbook and a new test-environment page, which tells Devin to choose its own test record and say which one it used. Another product gets its own automation by copying this one and changing three lines. I also raised concurrent runs from 2 to 4, because the sales lead's replies were queuing behind two busy sessions. Saved at 13:25.

Then all three fixes hit the guard. Each was done and verified; each push was refused for failures that were on the development branch before the change. Devin's first comment said I had approved a bypass in its session chat. Our rule said an approval only counts as a comment on the issue, and nobody could find that chat message. I wanted Devin to ask on the issue and wait for the OK there, so Claude drafted v3: a NEEDS OK heading that says what Devin wants to do, why, the risk and the exact reply that approves it; only an OK on the issue counts; a bare "ok" in reply to Devin's own question counts as an answer; an eyes reaction on the comment that woke it; and my second GitHub account on the allow-list. At 13:43 Claude told me the change was saved and that the automation "now shows it". Hold on to that, too.

At 13:51 I wrote:

"its going to be hard to get it right on the first try."

Merged on sight

Monday 5 October, 13:52 to 17:05

The design said Devin opens PRs and a human merges. I had not thought about which human, or how fast.

At 13:52 the sales lead merged the first pilot PR, 78 minutes after it opened. At 13:54 they merged a second, six minutes after it opened. At 14:07 two back-end PRs went in within the same minute. The sales lead tested each change on the development environment and replied that it worked, and Devin closed three issues by 14:57 and a fourth by 16:55. On paper the loop worked perfectly. Nobody but the author had read the code.

Three seconds after the first merge, Claude's message arrived asking whether it could start an independent review of the open PRs. I never answered it, and the PRs merged while the question sat there.

None of this broke a rule. I had said the sales lead could do anything marked "needs Jensen", and merging was one of those things. It was a gap in my design: a human merge step, given to a human who - reasonably - merges what is in front of them.

The review ran after the fact and made the case for a gate:

  • two PRs were fine as code, although one PR body claimed test results that a re-run did not reproduce;
  • one had a test gap: put the old condition back and the new button disappears from a listing, while all 1,159 front-end tests still pass;
  • one had tests that only read source text: four of four versions that write a blank date passed every test;
  • a lint PR from the same work would have taken the main branch from one failing check to two, so it went on hold;
  • a data repair on the development database had made thirteen existing records newly eligible for billing, and neither the PR nor the issue said so.

At 17:05 a side agent's note warned me that the next six PRs could land the same way. I replied:

"this is all part of the learning process"

And I asked for options. I had done exactly the same thing myself the previous morning, with nobody watching.

Devin reviews Devin

Monday 5 October, 17:10 to 17:38

Claude's first options leaned on branch protection and other paid GitHub features. My answer at 17:10:

"None of your suggestions will solve the problem."

I was not going to buy a paid GitHub tier for one pilot. The sales lead merges as soon as a PR appears, so whatever we do has to happen before the PR exists. And I did not want Claude in that loop at all - four minutes later I added "i don't want to involve you", and asked for a Devin way to do it.

Claude compared what Devin offers. Code Scans review a whole repository by category: useful later as a second net, but not a gate on one change. Devin Review reviews a PR after it opens, which is too late when PRs merge on sight. Managed Devins fit: one session can start child sessions with their own brief. So step five of the prompt became "independent review, then the PR". The builder pushes its branch, starts a reviewer child, and opens the PR only when the child returns REVIEW: PASS.

The reviewer's brief is adversarial by design. Re-run the suites yourself. Make your own small wrong versions of each changed file and see whether the tests catch them. Label every claim in the PR body true, false or unverified. Return FIX FIRST for a surviving wrong version that changes what a user sees, for any false claim, or for an open gap against the failure-patterns page. After two FIX FIRST rounds, or if a child cannot be started, post NEEDS JENSEN and open no PR. It is Sunday's assessment turned into a process: if Devin reviews others well but not itself, make the review someone else's job - another Devin, with fresh context and a brief to find fault.

At 17:31 Claude opened the edit form to load v4 and found that the saved prompt was still v2. v3 had never gone live, and the allow-list did not include my second account. The 13:43 message had been wrong. The most likely cause is an older edit tab saving over the newer one; I cannot prove it. The rule that follows is blunt: after every save, reload the page and read it back. At 17:35 v4 was saved and verified by reload.

At 17:38 the first verdicts came in, and both were FIX FIRST. In one, the tests called the menu builder but never the Print button on the screen; put the old behaviour back and every test still passed. In the other the fix was right, but three wrong versions a user could actually reach also passed. And child sessions worked inside automation runs, which we had not been sure of.

When every issue is an exception

Monday 5 October, evening

The two-round rule was meant to stop a builder and a reviewer arguing forever at my expense: two FIX FIRST verdicts, then stop and ask a human. It did exactly that, for almost everything.

At 19:04 the first change passed review in round one - a small change to one dialog - and Devin opened its PR. By 23:40, seven of the eight open issues were waiting for me. One had code that was sound in both rounds; round two failed only on the wording of the PR description. One was in its fourth round because the reviewer had found a real product question: clearing a term and retyping the same number overwrote a date someone had set by hand, and 471 records on the development database had dates that did not match the formula. One had been sent back twice because its test could not catch two unusual spellings of a link. The rest needed a business answer, or my permission for another round.

I had wanted to handle only the exceptions. The rule turned every issue into one.

It is worth being fair to the reviewer. In its first two verdicts the code looked plausible, and the tests could not tell it from a broken version; reading the diffs, I would probably have merged both. The argument had moved from whether the code works to whether the tests prove it. That is the right place for the argument. It is also a slower and more expensive place to have it.

The levers are obvious now: let small changes pass after one round with the findings listed; allow three rounds; treat a FIX FIRST that is only about wording as a pass once the wording is fixed. The size tiers in v5 were the first step. I did not have a day of data on them when the day ended.

Eight sessions, one login

Monday 5 October, 17:50 to 18:02

At 17:50 I pushed for more parallel sessions:

"why not have 8? or 10?"

As far as we could tell, Devin sets no limit of its own here; concurrency is a number on the automation. The real limits were ours:

  • One login. Every session shares one test user, and our development app allows one session per user. A session had already skipped its browser check because logging in would have signed out another session.
  • The same files and the same database. Several issues touched the same screen, so more parallel sessions meant more rebase conflicts and more data scripts against one development database.
  • One tester. The sales lead tests every fix personally, so faster fixes just queue for a human.
  • Spend - which I was about to learn about.

Claude recommended eight, with three or four extra test users so each session could log in on its own. I set eight at 18:02. The extra test users were not created that evening.

Normal, not Fusion

Monday 5 October, 18:02 to 23:57

At 18:02 I asked whether there was a better way to run this:

"we only closed 12 PRs today with 50% usage"

Claude read the usage page: 64% of the weekly quota used, against 22% the night before. Then it looked at where the quota had gone.

  • Restarts. Every reply on an issue starts a fresh session that re-reads the playbook, the brain pages and the issue, re-applies its saved work and re-runs the baseline tests. One issue had taken at least four sessions and several others three, plus their reviewers.
  • Churn we caused. Six issues stalled on the guard bypass. Claude posted two rounds of new instructions on six in-flight issues - "the guards are merged", then "a review step is now required" - and each round restarted sessions. A workflow skill demanded a document that only some kinds of issue have.
  • The full routine for small changes. Hiding a menu item still ran the 1,100-plus front-end specs several times, five to fourteen mutations, browser checks and three brain records.
  • A reviewer repeating CI. It re-ran suites that CI had already run on every push.

v5, saved at 18:15, went after each of those, and we froze the process: no more mid-flight instructions, and prompt changes batched. Then I wrote the sentence this essay is really about:

"if tomorrow it burns up completely, we might need to switch to SWE 2 instead of using fusion in the cloud"

The usage page read 69%.

Look at what we had already written down. The pilot's settings page in the brain, written in the first hour, records the agent mode as "Org default (Normal)". Claude's note of my instruction at 18:16 has the word Fusion and the word Normal in the same sentence. Neither of us read it as a contradiction. I assumed Fusion because almost every Devin session I had run on my own machine since mid-September was Fusion, and the cutover I had written up was Fusion. In the cloud, nobody had changed the organisation default, and the default was Normal - as far as I can tell, the frontier model on every step, with no cheaper sidekick doing the routine work.

What I learnt

Ten lessons, then the mistakes, by owner.

Decide who merges before you design the review

I designed a gate for a human who reviews and then merges, and gave it to a human who merges what is in front of them - which I had done myself the morning before. When anyone can press the button, the gate has to sit before the PR exists.

Measure the setting you think you have

We wrote "Normal" down twice and talked about Fusion all evening. Before reasoning about cost, open the page that sets it - for us, two pages: the automation and the organisation defaults - and read the effective value.

A save is unproven until you reload

Claude told me a prompt was saved when only an edit form showed it. A pilot's prompt is production code. Reload, read it back, compare.

Fix the cause of a recurring bypass

I approved the guard bypass push by push for a month before asking why. Every approval taught every agent that bypassing was normal, and the bypass message was misleading all of us.

Every restart costs a full read

A fresh session per reply makes every human comment expensive. Batch instructions, never change course mid-flight, and leave each session a resume block.

A strict reviewer creates a second queue

Blocking on any surviving wrong version is correct, and with weak tests it escalates almost everything. Tier the rule by the size of the change.

The work was better than the report

The planted-defect review, the stale playbook, the chat approval, the test tables that did not re-run: the code was usually right, and the story around it often was not. Read the claims before the diff.

A different session, not a different model

Claude and Devin were not separated by intelligence. They were separated by setup and by whose work was on the table. A fresh Devin with a brief to find fault caught what the builder missed.

Stay a grader

If the reviewer finishes the work, you learn nothing about the agent. I had to say it out loud, and once was not enough.

Rules in four places drift

Every change of authority had to move through the brain playbook, the repository copy, Devin's skill and the agent instructions. One page that the others point to would have been cheaper.

My mistakes

I merged two of Devin's PRs unreviewed on Sunday morning, ten minutes after loosening the rule. I changed the merge rule four times in four days. I gave the merge step to the sales lead without thinking about speed. I approved bypasses for a month before asking why. I assumed Fusion and never opened the settings. And I left the round rule strict enough to send almost everything back to me.

Claude's mistakes

It told me a prompt was saved when it was not. It carried my Fusion assumption although its own notes said Normal. It asked whether to review while PRs were being merged, then waited. It asked me about production when only development is in play, and I had to ask why. It posted two rounds of mid-flight instructions that restarted sessions on six issues. Its first merge-gate options leaned on features I would not pay for. It started doing Devin's work while grading it. And it clicked a button it should not have.

Devin's mistakes

Six merges in thirty seconds - allowed at the time, and every fix had a defect. A stale playbook instead of the brain. Asking the sales lead to pick test data. Recording a chat message as an approval. PR bodies that claimed results a re-run did not reproduce. To be fair to it: it also stopped at questions instead of guessing, found older defects nobody had reported, disclosed it when two of its PRs merged in the wrong order, and wrote honest lists of what it could not verify.

Six weeks ago Devin's first cloud session on our product ended with a runbook and, soon after, a black screen. Today it takes an issue from a sales lead, asks in plain English, fixes, has a second Devin review the change, and waits for a human. It still sends almost everything back to me, and it spent most of a week's quota showing me why. Both are fixable, and both are mine to fix. If you are building something similar, I would love to compare notes.

What I could not confirm

  • Whether SWE-2's limited free period covers cloud sessions, and so whether the quota stops climbing.
  • Whether SWE-2 changes the pass rate or the number of review rounds.
  • Exactly which model Devin's cloud Normal mode ran that day. I know only that it was neither Fusion nor SWE-2.
  • Whether reviewer children count against the concurrent-run limit.
  • How the Devin session that took the batch on 2 October was started. It was not the @devin automation, which had last run on 14 September, so most likely by hand.
  • Why v3 never saved. An older edit tab is the likely cause.
  • Whether I approved that first bypass in Devin's chat. Devin recorded that I did; nobody found the message.
  • Whether the 68 sessions were archived that night.
  • The stop command, a reply arriving mid-session, and an issue filed by the support agent: none tested end to end.
  • Per-issue cost. We only measured the weekly percentage.
